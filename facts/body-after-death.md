# The body after death — the post-mortem timeline

What happens to the physical body after death is among the best-understood processes in all of biology and forensic science. The timings below are *approximate* — temperature, body mass, clothing, cause of death, and environment all shift them — but the sequence is reliable and reproducible. Forensic pathologists use these markers to estimate time of death. [Saukko & Knight; DiMaio]

## The "mortis" markers `[ESTABLISHED]`

| Marker | What it is | Rough onset | Notes |
|--------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **Pallor mortis** | Paleness as blood drains from capillaries | ~15–30 min | Earliest, hardest to use forensically |
| **Algor mortis** | Body cooling to ambient temperature | Immediate, ongoing | Often cited near ~0.8 °C/hr but highly variable; depends on environment, body mass, clothing |
| **Livor mortis** (lividity) | Blood settles by gravity, purple-red pooling in dependent areas | ~20 min–3 hr; **fixed** by ~8–12 hr | Position of pooling and blanching reveals if/when a body was moved |
| **Rigor mortis** | Muscle stiffening as ATP depletes and actin-myosin locks | Onset ~2–6 hr; peak ~12 hr; resolves ~24–48 hr | Driven by biochemistry; accelerated by heat and prior exertion |

These four are the classic forensic quartet. None of them require anything mysterious — they are straightforward consequences of stopped circulation, cooling, gravity, and the cell's loss of the energy currency (ATP) that keeps muscle relaxed.

## Decomposition stages `[ESTABLISHED]`

Once cellular death is widespread, two processes drive breakdown:

- **Autolysis** — self-digestion. The cell's own enzymes, no longer regulated, begin breaking it down from the inside.
- **Putrefaction** — microbial action. The body's own gut microbiota (and environmental microbes) consume tissue, releasing gases.

The commonly described stages of decomposition (timings vary enormously with temperature, moisture, insects, and burial/exposure):

1. **Fresh** — autolysis underway; outwardly intact. Begins at death.
2. **Bloat** — gut bacteria produce gases (methane, hydrogen sulfide, etc.); the abdomen distends, skin discolors. Days.
3. **Active decay** — tissues liquefy and the body loses most mass; strong odor; insect activity (blowfly larvae) peaks. Days to weeks.
4. **Advanced decay** — most soft tissue gone. Weeks to months.
5. **Skeletonization / dry remains** — bone, cartilage, and dried tissue remain. Months to years; bone can persist far longer.

Forensic entomology (insect succession on a body) and these stages together allow surprisingly precise post-mortem-interval estimation. This is settled, applied science — none of it is in dispute.
